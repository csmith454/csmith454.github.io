Economic sanctions are one of the most important tools in the modern policy maker’s toolbox.  They have exploded in usage in the 21st century with the story of the literature telling of scholarly optimism rebuffed by continuous pessimism.  In the 20th century, this unclear estimation stemmed from problems with defining success, measuring success, and a lack of understanding the externalities of sanction implementation.  These problems persist to some degree in today’s changing world, but large steps towards solving them regularly arrive as the literature enjoys a rare, iterative connection with practitioners (Drezner 2023, 11).  The economic sanctions literature is by no means without further issues to solve, however.  Scholars writing literature reviews of their own point to a lack of understanding in the global economic effects of sanctions (Drezner 2023, 19), limited time-series data, and minimal non-state actor analysis (Peksen 2019, 641-2).  While trade and financial sanctions grow in uses and users, scholarship has similarly expanded, unveiling truths and myriad puzzles all the same.

**Overview**

The history of the economic sanctions literature begins in the post-World War II era.  Studies of high-profile cases dominated as theory grew.  The landmark study of this era, Johan Galtung’s analysis of the economic sanctions implemented on the Smith regime in Rhodesia, signaled the scholarly identification of a sanction’s purpose and the creation of a typology for their use.   Beyond the denial and limiting of trade for punishment of the target regime, economic sanctions also serve as political signals, where the sender reiterates the ideals of the target constituency (Galtung 1967, 404).  Following discussions of case studies, scholars required more data.  In the next wave, large-N studies flourished.  It was in this period that Hufbauer, Schott, and Elliot (HSE) published the foundational text of economic sanction theory: Economic Sanctions Reconsidered.  The original 1990 database was the first large-N study of sanctions, and found their included cases to be successful 34% of the time (HSE 2007, 158).  This study has been followed by the Threat and Imposition of Sanctions (TIES) dataset from Morgan et al. in 2009, the UN Targeted Sanctions Dataset from Biersteker et al. in 2016, and the Global Sanctions Data Base (GSDB) from Felbermayr et al. in 2020.

With increased access to information, the most recent era of scholarship discusses more theoretical questions and methodological strides.  For example, researchers investigate the effectiveness of the threat of economic sanctions, finding that just the threat of sanctions is a successful form of economic coercion (Drezner 2003, Walentak et al. 2021).  Furthermore, while scholars investigated methodology since the earlier waves,1 studies of this wave have taken advantage of a wealth of information from sanctions literature and other academic silos to pioneer new methods in order to find out why sanctions succeed (Jing et al. 2003, Bapat et al. 2013, Kavakli et al. 2020).  As sanctions become a ubiquitous tool of great powers globally, rather than reserved for Western use, the study of ‘national styles’ of economic statecraft has flourished as an important question in need of answering (Aggarwal and Reddie 2021).  

**Sanction Effectiveness in the Literature**

One of the debates in the literature is that of how to define sanction effectiveness.  HSE’s foundational work, originally released in 1985, with the third edition released in 2007, set the standard: success was to be defined by changes in the target country’s policies, behavior, or regime (HSE 2007, 7).  David Baldwin argued for a looser definition in Economic Statecraft (1985, 32), while Robert A. Pape added a few conditions to HSE’s definition, increasing the rigor: “(1) the target state conceded to a significant part of the coercer's demands; (2) economic sanctions were threatened or actually applied before the target changed its behavior; and (3) no more-credible explanation exists for the target's change of behavior.” (1997, 97).  Importantly, Baldwin added further nuance to how success should be defined.  Rather than solely defining it through sender-biased achievement of foreign policy goals, he believed that to make the literature relevant to policy makers the definition of success should also include the costs of achievement per the logic of choice.  Economic sanctions are only one tool in the policy maker’s kit, and because there is a choice to be made, the costs must be weighed against the alternatives (Baldwin 1999, 106).  Recent studies incorporate discussion of costs while adhering to the standard set by HSE (Biersteker et al. 2016, Rosenburg et al. 2016, Peksen 2019, Drezner 2023). 
 
With this debate emphatically engaging the literature at the turn of the century, another equally topical debate looms: do economic sanctions work?  The goal of the previously discussed large-N studies was to answer this exact question.  While HSE found 34% of cases to be successful, Pape’s critique crushed their estimate to 10%, criticizing their criteria for success and evaluation of invalid cases (1997).  While success is a hot topic, the literature generally agrees that economic sanctions are successful under certain conditions, with these debates well-summarized by Peksen’s literature review.  The use of economic sanctions in a multilateral manner, against democratic allies, and for less ambitious policy goals succeed in coercing more often than those used independently against non-democratic rivals.  Furthermore, well-crafted sanctions that do more economic damage are more successfully in achieving partial or full acquiescence to demands.  Lastly, while targeted sanctions have been sold as the more humanitarian solution, they do not have any more success than blanket trade and financial sanctions (2019, 637-40).  Many exceptions exist due to the contextual nature of sanction application.  For example, financial sanctions that deny the target access to lines of credit are particularly effective even when used against personalist regimes (Rosenburg et al. 2016).  Ultimately, the literature is gathering a clearer picture of the effectiveness of economic sanctions, with recent studies capturing the casual mechanisms more clearly (Bapat et al. 2013, Bělín and Hanousek 2021).  As we further understand mechanisms and devise valid methods to measure success, scholarship is shifting to understand their externalities.

**Outcomes and Futures**

While the literature generally agrees on economic sanctions as a cost-effective substitute for military force and diplomatic action, the  evidence demands a formal reevaluation of how cost-effective they might be.  Sanction regimes, and particularly targeted sanctions that go after specific interest groups and financial actors, are thought to be less harmful to the target’s populace than military force.  Although this may be true, economic violence is still violence.  Scholarship has found that UN sanction regimes (often supported by multilateral great power sanctions) have multiple negative externalities.  Disease and death occurs at an increased rate after the imposition of UN sanctions.  Furthermore, this causation is gendered.  Women are more impacted by the negative effects of UN sanctions; lifespans decrease by an average of one year after their implementation and more for women in the target country (Drury and Peksen 2014, Biersteker et al. 2016, Gutmann et al. 2021).  Beyond disease and death, economic sanctions also have effects on corruption and informal economies in targeted countries.  After the implementation of sanctions, corruption increases, crime increases, and informal economies expand.  Additionally, these effects do not dissipate after successful coercion, they last (Andreas 2005, Biersteker et al. 2016, Early and Peksen 2019).  The effect is uniform for targeted sanctions as well (Biersteker et al. 2016, Rosenburg et al. 2016).

Effectiveness and externalities of sanctions are both explored by the literature, but midst the economic reorientation of the global north, new gaps emerge and old gaps persist.  The definition of success for sanctions may not need to be reworked, but in light of statistically significant studies that find negative, long-lasting externalities from economic sanctions, the importance of costs to the definition should be reevaluated.  Economic sanctions work, but at what cost?  It is clear that harm to populations within the target jurisdiction is present, but the externalities that harm the sender population (and global economics as a whole as Drezner (2023, 19) argues) are knowingly complex and hard to estimate.  Scholars should expand efforts on this challenge immediately.  Furthermore, as Peksen argues, the focus on non-state actors in studies of economic sanctions is minimal (2019, 641-2).  Targeted sanctions of the 21st century have increased in granularity, to the point that individual oil tankers must be monitored for compliance with a sanction regime, yet the scholarly focus on the granularity of economic sanctions has not followed suit.  Scholars should deal with questions of success and partial success from the most sweeping sanctions to the most minute.  

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, sanctions are effective.  Datasets new and old show effectiveness between 22% and 40%, and scholars have narrowed in on what contextual specifics create better outcomes.  However, their outcomes and externalities create understudied global issues that are highly relevant to an increasingly fragmented global economy.  Drezner notes this fragmentation and alludes to the economic blocs of the Cold War as a potential future (2023, 17).  Economic sanctions are a necessary alternative to military force, but policy makers should be under no illusion that they are ‘humanitarian,’ rather they are a tool reshaping the global economy for better and for worse.  As policy makers of the 21st century reach for this tool more with less success (i.e. mounting sanctions without changes in the target regime), the definitions and measurement of economic sanctions changes.  Scholars should reevaluate the age old question: do economic sanctions work?
