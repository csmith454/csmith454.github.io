What is in a name?  Attaching 'shadow' to a normally legal activity renders it illegal, illicit; but the branding may not tell the more nuanced story lying underneath such a shiny veneer.  It argues for an image of organized crime, corruption, and weak institutions.  Shadow as a prefix carries the inherent meaning of being outside the purview of laws and norms.  However, in preceding 'fleet' the newly crafted term arrives at a paradox: the shadowfleet is well within the purview of laws and norms, yet it semantically denies such an existence. The shadowfleet as an arena of maritime trade is an institution, but semantically exists outside of the system.  Marketing such a ubiquitous and essential activity of global markets as illegal does more than shock.  It denies consumers, participants, and researchers an appreciation for systemic complicity.  

I recently read a 2006 report on trade-based money laundering by the OECD's Financial Action Task Force. They define trade-based money laundering as "...disguising the proceeds of crime and moving value through the use of trade transactions in an attempt to legitimise their illicit origins." The authors discuss how criminal organizations and terrorist financiers utilize international trade to launder profits from illicit industries.  One case study stood out to me: a Brazilian company operating illicit industries smuggles cash out to shell banks.  These banks fund shell companies that then buy inflated product from the Brazilian company's legal trade.  The shell companies offload the inflated product at a much discounted price and remit the now-legal tender back to Brazil.  These activities could be labeled 'shadow.'  However, these activities are dissimilar to the actions of the so-called shadowfleet.  A state attempting to sell its indigenously-produced resource according to global trade institutions would normally not be described in such a fashion.  Trade-based money laundering is regularly reserved for non-state actors.

Now the last thing I want to do is sanction the Kremlin's funding of war crimes and terror through its energy resources or support North Korea's export of goods to fund a missile program.  Rather, I want to highlight the interesting and somewhat paradoxical existence of the shadowfleet in the international trade infrastructure.  The shadowfleet is subjectively pariah-ed on the global stage due to its nation-state relations and in spite of its private operation.  A private operation working in illicit, trade-based money laundering is a global threat to state actors, but a 'privately'-owned shadowfleet with potential state associations cannot be categorized as the same despite its colloquial name.

 I think the relative magic behind the economic and political characteristics of such a naval resource is the plausible deniability afforded to actors at points of sale.  Sanctions and secondary sanctions have limited power unless targets truly believe the costs they will bear cannot be avoided and/or will be too great.  Therefore, if private actors can avoid the consequences because a shipment's origins are sufficiently masked and the state does not feel coerced to enforce (read: China and India) then a legal sale will take place across ledgers.  Thus, the resource and funds have been legitimized at an unenforceable pace through systemic and state facilitation.  It is this idiosyncrasy of the 'shadow'-fleet that denies itself the prefix of discussion.  
 
 What is to be done?  The name will stick around.  It will continue to cast a masking shadow on systemic realities.  Illegal trade will continue to grow complex and normalized.  The use of sanctions, both primary and secondary, will increase, and states will partition global trade more than it has in the last quarter-century.  I think the most important thing we can do is learn more.  My Master's thesis will engage directly with this topic by crafting a process trace of Russia's crude oil supply chain with an eye to the Kremlin's sanction evasion infrastructure.  The shadowfleet directly impacts trade at the midstream, and I want to understand the causal chain of pressure that sanctions create.
